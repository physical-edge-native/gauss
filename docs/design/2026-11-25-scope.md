# Scope of gauss

Date: 2026-11-25. Issue: #7. Decided by the maintainer; this record was written with an AI assistant.

## Decision

gauss compares two ways of controlling the same legged robot, **as a question of model architecture**:

- **split control**: one controller per leg. Each controller sees only its own leg's sensors and the values
  exchanged with other legs, following the [Physical-Edge Native Architecture](https://physical-edge-native.github.io/)
  (regions that exchange only values on their borders);
- **central control**: one policy that sees the whole body.

The comparison is made **in simulation only**, on four-legged robots **of the size of industrial robots**, and is
trained **on CPUs**. gauss makes no claim that split control is better than central control. The measured
differences, including where split control is worse, are the result. As in erg, a specific difference may be claimed
only if it was written down before the experiment, in a separate record, and is reported with what split control
costs or loses.

gauss also answers the question handed over by erg (erg `docs/design/2026-11-24-scope.md`, erg#10): **whether split
control lowers the computation needed for training.**

## The robots: A2 size first, Go2 size as an intermediate step

| Robot | Mass | Joints | Model used | License of the model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unitree A2 (main robot) | about 35 kg without battery, about 42 kg with battery ([Unitree](https://unitree.com/A2)); the parts of the MJCF model add up to about 40 kg | 12 (three per leg); joint torque limits 120 N·m (hip, thigh) and 180 N·m (knee) in the model | `unitree_robots/a2` in [unitree_mujoco](https://github.com/unitreerobotics/unitree_mujoco) (URDF in [unitree_ros](https://github.com/unitreerobotics/unitree_ros)) | BSD-3-Clause (Unitree Robotics) |
| Unitree Go2 (intermediate size) | about 15 kg | 12 (three per leg) | `unitree_go2` in [MuJoCo Menagerie](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_menagerie), derived from Unitree's URDF | BSD-3-Clause (Unitree Robotics) |

Why:

- erg's robot (about 1.5 kg), Go2 (about 15 kg) and A2 (about 40 kg) all have **four legs with three joints each**.
  The body plan stays the same while the size grows about ten times at each step, so differences that appear only
  at industrial size can be separated from differences caused by the body plan.
- A2 is sold as an industrial quadruped (inspection and carrying loads), so it stands for "industrial size". Go2 is
  the common research quadruped between erg's robot and A2, with a large body of prior work to check our setup
  against.
- Both have public MuJoCo models under a permissive license. How the model files are brought into gauss (copied
  with their license, or fetched at a fixed commit) is decided in the first implementation issue.
- If the CPU budget is not enough for both, A2 alone is run, and Go2 is dropped first.

The exact mass, torque limits and parameters used are recorded with each experiment; the robots are not modified
to look like erg's robot.

Other options considered: a humanoid of industrial size (more limbs, but a different body plan, so the size effect
could not be separated); an industrial arm (split per joint, and the task changes from walking to manipulation);
several robot types at once (too many runs for the CPU budget).

## The architectures compared

On the same robot, task and training budget, along a ladder from fully separate to fully central (the same ladder
as erg, plus a structured model in between):

1. **no messages**: one controller per leg, coordination only through the body;
2. **neighbours only**: each leg exchanges a short vector with the leg beside it and the leg in front of or behind
   it (the ring front-left – front-right – hind-right – hind-left – front-left);
3. **all legs**: each leg exchanges with all three others;
4. **body-structured central model**: one network over the whole body whose layers connect only neighbouring parts
   (a graph network or masked attention, as in prior work below); central in where it runs, split in its structure;
5. **central policy**: one network that sees all sensors (a multilayer perceptron, the usual baseline).

Per-leg controllers share their weights across the four legs (with a leg identifier as input), as in erg. Message
contents and sizes follow erg's design (body orientation estimate, foot contact, a short learned vector) where they
apply, so the two repositories can be compared.

## The task

Walking that follows a commanded velocity (forward, sideways, turning) on flat ground and on rough ground not seen in
training, with a control period of 20 ms (50 Hz), the period used by erg and common for quadrupeds of this size.
Jumps, flips and other acrobatic motion are out of scope (pena-meta decision of 2026-11-25).

## What is measured

All numbers per architecture and robot, with the same training budget (environment steps) and at least 5 training
seeds each; the mean and the spread are reported, never the best seed alone.

- **Task**: velocity-tracking error, falls per minute, on flat and on unseen rough ground.
- **Training computation**: environment steps and CPU hours until a set walking quality is first reached, and at the
  end of the budget; with the machine of every run recorded (CPU model, number of threads, memory).
- **Computation per controller**: parameters and multiply-adds per control step, per leg and for the whole robot.
- **Communication**: bytes per control step per leg and for the whole robot.
- **Faults**: message loss (0–30 %) and delay (0–2 control steps, with jitter), one leg's controller stopping (its
  leg holds its last position) and coming back; one leg's sensors frozen or noisy.
- **Scaling with size**: how each of the above changes from Go2 size to A2 size (and, where erg has the same
  measurement, from erg's size).

Acceptance of the tools (not of a "winner"): the same seed gives identical simulation outputs on every run, and
message loss and delay are reproducible from a seed.

## Simulation and compute

- **MuJoCo** on CPUs, many simulation instances in parallel processes; policies trained with a standard on-policy
  algorithm (PPO) in Python.
- The maintainer has **CPUs only** for now. Training legged robots of this size is usually done on a GPU with
  thousands of parallel environments; on CPUs each run takes longer, so the number of runs is planned:
  1. first measure simulation throughput (environment steps per second) of A2 and Go2 on the training machine;
  2. fix one training budget in environment steps for all architectures;
  3. run the central policy and the neighbours-only split first, then the rest of the ladder, A2 before Go2;
  4. record the CPU hours of every run, including failed ones.
- If a GPU becomes available later, the same code and budgets are kept, and the hardware is recorded with every run.
- **Where training runs.** Code is written and checked in cloud sessions and in CI, as in every repository of the
  organization. Long training runs are run on the **maintainer's own PC**: the cloud sessions have few threads (4 on
  2026-11-25) and are reclaimed after a period of inactivity, so runs of many hours would be cut off. The results and
  their records (configuration, seed, CPU hours, the machine) are committed to this repository. The CPU of that PC is
  recorded when the throughput is first measured. The shared rule that only work needing hardware is done on a local
  PC (pena-meta `cross-repo-rules.md`) does not mention long training runs; a clarification is proposed in pena-meta.

## Message loss and delay: modelled in Python, no dependency on Dyne at first

Message loss, delay and jitter between leg controllers are modelled in Python with a seeded random generator.
gauss does not depend on [Dyne](https://github.com/physical-edge-native/dyne) at first: the question is about model
architecture, not about running on devices. Depending on Dyne later (for example its seeded virtual network or its
learned-solver format) would add a row to the pinned commits in pena-meta `contracts.md`, so it would be proposed in
pena-meta first.

## Prior work this builds on

Most prior comparisons are on small simulated bodies with perfect communication; we found none at industrial size
with message loss and delay (a first search, not a full survey).

- Per-leg controllers on a simulated four-legged agent learned faster than a central controller and generalized
  better; the best generalization to uneven ground came from controllers that use only information from neighbouring
  legs: M. Schilling, A. Melnik, F. W. Ohl, H. J. Ritter, B. Hammer, "Decentralized control and local information for
  robust and adaptive decentralized Deep Reinforcement Learning", *Neural Networks* 144 (2021)
  ([PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/34673323/)).
- The same comparison for a hexapod: M. Schilling et al., "Decentralized Deep Reinforcement Learning for a Distributed
  and Adaptive Locomotion Controller of a Hexapod Robot" (2020) ([arXiv:2005.11164](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11164));
  a hierarchical version for a four-legged agent: W. Zai El Amri, L. Hermes, M. Schilling (2022)
  ([arXiv:2210.08003](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.08003)).
- Per-leg policies for articulated robots that learned where a central one did not converge, and transferred to a
  robot with a failed leg: G. Sartoretti et al., "Distributed Learning of Decentralized Control Policies for
  Articulated Mobile Robots" (2019) ([arXiv:1901.08537](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08537)).
- Groups of weakly connected motors controlled separately on a quadruped and a humanoid, robust to faults that spread
  through one coupled network: Y. Guo et al., "Decentralized Motor Skill Learning for Complex Robotic Systems" (2023)
  ([arXiv:2306.17411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17411)).
- One shared module per actuator with message passing between modules: W. Huang, I. Mordatch, D. Pathak, "One Policy
  to Control Them All" (ICML 2020) ([arXiv:2007.04976](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04976)).
- A body-structured central model (attention only between neighbouring parts of the body), reported better than a
  multilayer perceptron and a plain transformer: C. Sferrazza et al., "Body Transformer" (CoRL)
  ([arXiv:2408.06316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06316)).
- A standard benchmark that splits a simulated body into several agents: Multi-agent MuJoCo, introduced with FACMAC,
  B. Peng et al. (2021) ([arXiv:2003.06709](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.06709)); small bodies only.

## Other options considered

- **Build hardware as well.** Would need an industrial-size robot; the architecture question does not need it, and
  erg covers hardware.
- **Use Dyne from the start.** Brings its seeded network and bit-exact execution, but adds a cross-repository
  dependency before the architecture question needs it.
- **Wait for a GPU.** Delays the start; the CPU budget is planned instead and the hardware is recorded with each run.
